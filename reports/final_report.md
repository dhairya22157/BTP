# Quantitative Evaluation of Embedding Models for Protocol Classification

## 1. Introduction
This report presents a comprehensive quantitative analysis of various embedding models utilized for the automated clustering and classification of cooking protocols. The evaluation compares traditional vectorization methods against advanced Transformer-based architectures to identify the optimal representation technique for domain-specific text.

## 2. Methodology
The evaluation is based on two primary dimensions:
1. **Clustering Quality (Adjusted Rand Index - ARI):** Measures the structural agreement between the automated clusters generated by the embeddings and the human-annotated ground truth classifications (comprising 31 distinct categories).
2. **Embedding Compactness (PCA Explained Variance):** Determines the minimum number of principal components required to retain at least **90% of the cumulative explained variance**, reflecting the density and efficiency of the latent semantic space.

The models evaluated include Sentence-BERT (SBERT), domain-specific variants (CookBERT, FoodBERT, BioBERT, PubMedBERT), and classical baselines (Word2Vec, Doc2Vec, TF-IDF).

## 4. Discussion & Comparative Analysis

### 4.1. The Failure of Classical Baselines
Traditional sparse representations like **TF-IDF** (ARI: -0.0243) and **Count Vectorizers** completely failed to abstract the semantic meaning of the protocols. TF-IDF suffered from massive dimensionality inflation, necessitating **243 components** just to reach 90% variance, while yielding worse-than-random clustering alignments. 
Similarly, while shallow neural embeddings like **Word2Vec** and **Doc2Vec** produced highly compact vector spaces (71 and 75 components, respectively), their semantic expression lacked the contextual depth required for complex instructional texts, resulting in near-zero ARI scores (~0.005).

### 4.2. Deep Contextual Models & Domain Specificity
The Transformer-based models demonstrated inherently superior capability in parsing instructional nuances. Domain-adapted models—specifically **CookBERT** (ARI: 0.1980) and **PubMedBERT** (ARI: 0.1854)—showed moderate success. Their performance indicates that while domain-specific pretraining provides an edge over generalized models like BioBERT (ARI: 0.1179), they remain constrained by structural limitations when processing lengthy or structurally rigid protocol formats.

### 4.3. Superiority of Sentence-BERT (SBERT)
**SBERT** comprehensively outperformed all other approaches. By achieving an ARI of **0.5848** (at K=37 with updated ground truth labels), it demonstrated an unparalleled ability to map structurally disparate yet semantically identical instructions into highly cohesive clusters. Furthermore, SBERT showcased excellent representational efficiency, requiring only **112 principal components** to map 90% of the dataset's variance. This indicates a deeply structured, linearly separable latent space that expertly balances expressiveness with compactness.

## 5. Conclusion
Based on both clustering efficacy and spatial compactness, **SBERT is definitively the optimal model** for classifying protocol data. It bridges the semantic gap that classical models fail to cross, and it significantly exceeds the alignment capabilities of even natively domain-adapted models like CookBERT. Future classification pipelines within this architecture should prioritize SBERT operating at $K \approx 37$ to align reliably with the 31 fundamental manual categories.
